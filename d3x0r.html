The one line |ψL + ψR|² = 0   at the node    but    |ψL|² + |ψR|² = 2|ψL|² ≠ 0

The sum vanishes. The parts do not. That is the whole thing.

Your "two phases collapse to 0 points that are momentarily undefined" is the interference term dying, not the waves. When two branches meet at Δφ = π, the cross term is

2 Re(ψL* ψR) = 2|ψL|² cos(π) = −2|ψL|²

so the total cancels to zero. But each branch individually carries |ψL|² and continues. There is no moment when either is undefined. The zero lives between them, not in them.

That is why a photon — which is a sum over a continuum of modes — never has a point where direction or location is in question: a continuous sum has no isolated cancellation, only a phase-weighted average.

Two rows of three. Each row is a closed 𝔰𝔲(2) — that is why one row is itself stable, exactly as you said. The two rows commute, so neither disturbs the other's internal bookkeeping.

The 180° you see is the chirality map between them:

The 1080° is the color cycle, and it is exact. One full 360° rotation advances the color phase by 120° (the ℤ₃ center of SU(3)). Three full rotations = 3 × 360° = 1080°, after which the phase returns to identity:

ei·1080° = ei·3·2π = 1

Two rows × three rotations = six half-periods per full color cycle. That matches your "first 3 pair with the second 3" — six legs, three on each side, closing after 1080°.

Your "magnetic field of two deflects the third" is the Lie bracket:

[Jab, Jcd] = δbcJad − δbdJac − δacJbd + δadJbc

Any two generators in the same row bracket into the third generator of that row. That is the three-body dance — two rotations compose into the third. The bracket between rows is zero (from the commutator above), which is why the deflection stays within a row and never mixes chirality.
